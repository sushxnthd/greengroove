green groove

A supermarket checkout experiment that became a systems project about identity, sensing and keeping physical and digital state aligned.

why we made it

We began with a simple observation: a supermarket already sees the moment a product leaves a shelf, but conventional checkout largely ignores that event and identifies the item again later.

Green Groove started as a product-design concept built around three pieces: an RFID-enabled shopper band, a sensor-assisted shelf and a digital cart. The early idea was mostly about convenience. The more interesting problem appeared when we tried to make the system internally coherent.

If a product moves, which shopper caused the movement? What happens when it is put back? What if the RFID evidence and the shelf signal disagree? Those questions pushed the project away from “smart object” design and toward systems engineering.

system

four hand-offs, one transaction.

The transaction is built gradually while the customer shops instead of being reconstructed entirely at the counter.

01identify

An RFID-enabled band establishes one persistent shopper session.

02sense

Shelf-side hardware detects a product being picked up or returned.

03associate

The physical event is linked to the correct shopper and updates the live cart.

04settle

The accumulated state becomes the basis for billing and inventory updates.

engineering notes

the questions that changed the project.

These were more useful than adding another feature or polishing another screen.

what happens when an item is returned?

A removal-only system is not enough. Returning an item has to reverse the exact cart state created by the original pick event. That made inverse events a first-class part of the model rather than an edge case.

what if two signals disagree?

RFID evidence and shelf-state change may not arrive at the same time or may point to different interpretations. A credible implementation should preserve uncertainty instead of silently converting every sensor event into a transaction.

what if two people act at once?

Nearby shoppers can create overlapping events. That means association logic matters as much as detection: the system needs to decide not only that something moved, but whose cart should change.

what actually needs to persist?

The shopper identity must survive across the whole trip. Re-inferring it at every shelf would make the system fragile and would recreate the same identification problem that checkout already has.

my work

across the project, not just one layer.

research. We mapped supermarket friction points and narrowed them into a system problem that could be designed and discussed concretely.

architecture. I helped shape the identity → shelf event → cart state → settlement flow and the logic around returns and ambiguous events.

interaction. I worked on how the band, shelf and digital cart should behave as one experience rather than three unrelated products.

prototyping and communication. I built the visual and product artifacts used to explain the concept, expose weaknesses and iterate on it.

what I took from it

The useful part of Green Groove was not the branding exercise. It was learning that a polished interaction only works when the invisible state underneath it is trustworthy.

The project made me think about what each component knows, when it knows it, what can go wrong, and how a physical action becomes reliable digital information. That systems perspective is the part I would keep developing now.
