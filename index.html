Student engineering + product design

Green Groove

A supermarket checkout experiment that became a systems project.

Two students · Gurugram, India

RFID identity · shelf sensing · live digital cart

Selected under NCERT PRAYAAS

The idea

We started with a simple question: why identify every item again at checkout if the store already saw it leave the shelf?

Green Groove began as a product-design concept built around three pieces: a shopper wristband, a smart shelf and a lightweight digital cart.

The more interesting problem appeared later. If the system was going to work at all, it needed to keep physical state and digital state aligned while people picked up, returned and moved products around a store.

How it works

Four hand-offs, one transaction.

  1. 01

    Identify the shopper

    An RFID-enabled band gives one shopper a persistent session identity.

  2. 02

    Observe the shelf

    Shelf-side hardware detects a product being picked up or returned.

  3. 03

    Update the cart

    The event changes the shopper’s digital cart while the trip is still happening.

  4. 04

    Settle at the end

    The accumulated state becomes the basis for billing and inventory updates.

shopper identity→shelf event→cart state→billing

The same path has to work in reverse when an item is returned.

Engineering notes

The device was never the hardest part.

The difficult questions were about state, ambiguity and failure.

Persistent identity

A shopper cannot be re-inferred every time something moves. The identity has to persist across the trip.

Design consequence: one session, many shelf events.

Returns are first-class events

Detecting removal is not enough. Putting an item back must reverse the exact state change that picking it up created.

Design consequence: every forward action needs an inverse.

Sensor disagreement

Real hardware can disagree. RFID evidence and weight change may not arrive together or may point to different interpretations.

Design consequence: uncertain events need to be flagged, not silently trusted.

Simultaneous actions

Two customers can reach for nearby products at almost the same time. The system cannot assume clean, isolated events.

Design consequence: association logic matters as much as detection.

The turning point

The first version was about a smart object. The better version was about keeping several states synchronized.

That change in framing is what made Green Groove useful to me as an engineering project. It stopped being a branding exercise and became a way to reason about a real physical-digital system.

What I took from it

The user experience is only as believable as the invisible logic underneath it.

Green Groove made me think less about isolated “smart” products and more about systems: what each component knows, when it knows it, what can go wrong, and how a physical event becomes trusted digital information.

That is the part of the project I would keep developing now.
